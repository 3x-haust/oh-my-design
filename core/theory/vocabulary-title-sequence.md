# Cross-domain vocabulary: title sequence

A working solution to "make a viewer want to keep watching for the next ninety seconds" that existed
before the web. Use this when a page must carry attention across a beginning rather than answer a
question immediately.

## What the material already solved

- **The cold open.** Attention is earned before anything is explained. A first frame is a promise of
  a register, not a summary of the contents.
- **Timing as hierarchy.** What arrives first, what waits, and what never appears is the composition.
  A title that lands after two seconds of held black carries more weight than any size increase.
- **Type in motion as the subject itself.** Letterforms can be the event, not a label on one.
- **The credit sequence's discipline.** Names given space, unadorned, in a fixed rhythm, with the
  background doing the atmospheric work. Nothing competes with the line being read.
- **Sound as a structural element.** A beat can mark a transition where a divider would otherwise be
  needed.

## What it offers a screen

- An entry that establishes register before asking anything of the reader.
- Sequential reveal used for legibility rather than decoration: one claim settled before the next
  arrives, so a scrolling page has a reading order instead of a list.
- Space held long enough to be felt. A pause is a design element the web consistently underuses.

## What not to take

Literal film furniture. Timecode, letterboxing, scanlines, and film grain read as costume. The
transferable part is restraint in sequence and confidence in holding a moment.

## Where it misleads

A title sequence plays once. Screen content is re-read, skipped, and returned to, so anything that
only works on first pass — an animation that carries meaning available nowhere else — breaks the
task. Motion may introduce; it must never be the only carrier of a requirement.
