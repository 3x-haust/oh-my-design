# Cross-domain vocabulary: signage system

A working solution to "tell a stranger where they are and what to do next, with no explanation" that
existed before the web. Use this when a screen must orient someone who has no context and will not
read carefully.

## What the material already solved

- **Legibility at a distance under bad conditions.** Weight, contrast, and letterform survive glare,
  motion, and haste. A wayfinding sign is tested against a worse situation than a desktop ever is.
- **The decision point.** A sign appears where a choice is made, not where it is convenient to place.
  This is placement as part of the information.
- **Consistent grammar across a whole system.** The same arrow, the same colour logic, the same type
  in every location, so one learned rule applies everywhere. Predictability is the feature.
- **Redundancy that is not noise.** Colour, shape, and text repeat the same distinction because any
  one channel can fail. This is deliberate, not decoration.
- **Destinations named as the traveller names them.** Signs use the word people actually say, not the
  internal name of the department that owns the place.

## What it offers a screen

- Orientation that does not depend on remembering a previous page.
- State and location carried by more than one channel, so colour is never the only signal.
- Labels that use the user's vocabulary rather than the system's internal nouns.
- Navigation placed where the decision actually happens.

## What not to take

Airport chrome. Brushed metal, pictogram grids, and the "transit system" palette are costume. The
transferable part is legibility under pressure and predictability across a system.

## Where it misleads

Signage is read by someone in motion with one goal. A screen is also operated: the user will press,
type, and undo things. Signage has no vocabulary for consequences, so anything destructive needs the
screen's own answers for confirmation and recovery.
