# Cross-domain vocabulary: scientific field guide

A working solution to "let a non-expert identify one specific thing among many similar ones" that
existed before the web. Use this when a screen must help someone distinguish, not merely browse.

## What the material already solved

- **The plate and the key.** A specimen is drawn once, at a consistent scale, with its distinguishing
  features marked. Comparison is the point, so all the candidates appear in one comparable field.
- **Consistent scale as an argument.** When a hummingbird and a heron appear in the same plate at true
  relative size, the reader learns something no caption would convey. Scale that shifts between items
  destroys the comparison silently.
- **Labelled anatomy with leader lines.** A feature is named where it is, not in a legend far away.
  This is the ancestor of a well-placed annotation.
- **Dichotomous keys.** A sequence of either/or questions that reliably lands a person on one answer.
  It is a decision tree with every branch published, and it is the honest form of a filter.
- **Density that serves identification.** A plate is dense because the reader must compare, so the
  density is functional rather than decorative.

## What it offers a screen

- Comparison as a first-class layout: candidates side by side, comparable, at a shared scale.
- Annotation attached to the thing it describes rather than collected elsewhere.
- A filtering flow that shows the reader where they are in the decision, the way a key does.

## What not to take

The cabinet-of-curiosities surface. Aged paper, copperplate scripts, and specimen frames read as
costume. The transferable part is the comparability and the honesty of scale.

## Where it misleads

A field guide assumes a patient reader with a goal. A screen often has seconds and a half-formed
intent, so a full key up front is hostile. Publish the decision honestly, but do not make the reader
walk every branch to answer a question they have not yet formed.
